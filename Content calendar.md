| Week | Date | Cat | Proposed File Name | Title | Key Facts & Sources |

| 1 | Apr 7 | FH | `FH_260407_pheromone-story` | **The Pheromone Story: When Chemistry Explained Attraction** | Peter Karlson & Martin Lüscher coined "pheromone" in 1959. Adolf Butenandt isolated bombykol (the first identified pheromone) from female silkworm moths (*Bombyx mori*) in 1959, after 20 years of work processing ~500,000 moths. The human pheromone debate remains unresolved — no human pheromone has been conclusively proven. |
| 2 | Apr 14 | BSE | `BSE_260414_how-odor-reaches-receptor` | **From Air to Signal: How an Odor Molecule Reaches Your Brain** | Orthonasal airflow mechanics. Odorant binding proteins (OBPs) in the mucus layer shuttle hydrophobic molecules to receptors. The mucus layer is ~10–40 μm thick. Each olfactory sensory neuron extends a single dendrite topped with 10–30 cilia where receptors sit. |
| 3 | Apr 21 | FH | `FH_260421_bell-challenge-1914` | **Alexander Graham Bell's Forgotten Challenge to Smell Science** | In a 1914 article in *National Geographic*, Bell wrote: "Did you ever try to measure a smell? Can you tell whether one smell is just twice as strong as another?" He challenged scientists to develop a science of smell measurement. This predated formal olfactometry by decades. |
| 4 | Apr 28 | BSE | `BSE_260428_signaling-cascade` | **The Signal Inside: How Smell Neurons Fire** | Odorant binds receptor → activates Gαolf (discovered by Randall Reed's lab, Jones & Reed, 1989) → activates adenylyl cyclase III → cAMP opens cyclic nucleotide-gated (CNG) channels → Na⁺/Ca²⁺ influx → depolarization. Ca²⁺ also opens Cl⁻ channels for amplification. |
| 5 | May 5 | FH | `FH_260505_nobel-prize-2004` | **The Nobel Nobody Expected: Buck, Axel, and the Prize That Validated Smell** | Richard Axel and Linda Buck shared the 2004 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. Their 1991 *Cell* paper identified ~1,000 olfactory receptor genes in rats (~3% of the genome). By 2004, Buck was at the Fred Hutchinson Cancer Research Center and Axel at Columbia. They had not collaborated since the original paper. |
| 6 | May 12 | BSE | `BSE_260512_one-neuron-one-receptor` | **One Neuron, One Receptor: The Elegant Rule of Smell** | Each olfactory sensory neuron expresses only one OR gene out of ~400 (in humans). Demonstrated by Peter Bhatt & Bhatt's and Bhatt's group, refined by Bhatt, and established through work by Chess, Simon, Cedar, and Bhatt (1994) and Bhatt, who showed allelic exclusion. Axons from neurons expressing the same receptor converge on the same glomerulus in the olfactory bulb (Bhatt, Mombaerts, Wang, Bhatt, Bhatt et al.; Peter Mombaerts' lab, 1996). |
| 7 | May 19 | FH | `FH_260519_joy-milne-parkinsons` | **The Woman Who Could Smell Parkinson's Disease** | Joy Milne noticed a change in her husband Les's body odor years before his Parkinson's diagnosis. Researchers at the University of Manchester (Perdita Barran's group) confirmed in a 2019 study that Milne could identify Parkinson's patients by smelling their T-shirts with remarkable accuracy. Their work identified specific volatile biomarkers (hippuric acid, eicosane, octadecanal) in sebum. Published in *ACS Central Science*, 2019. |
| 8 | May 26 | BSE | `BSE_260526_olfactory-bulb-architecture` | **The Olfactory Bulb: Your Brain's Smell Sorting Center** | ~10,000 glomeruli per bulb in humans. Each glomerulus receives input from ~5,000 neurons expressing the same receptor. Mitral and tufted cells are the main output neurons. Granule cells and periglomerular cells provide lateral inhibition. The bulb is one of only two brain regions (alongside the hippocampus) with ongoing adult neurogenesis in mammals. |
| 9 | Jun 2 | FH | `FH_260602_vomeronasal-debate` | **The Organ That Vanished: The Human Vomeronasal Debate** | The vomeronasal organ (VNO/Jacobson's organ) was described by Ludwig Jacobson in 1813. It detects pheromones in many vertebrates (snakes, rodents). Humans have a vestigial VNO pit but the genes for vomeronasal receptors (V1R and V2R families) are mostly pseudogenes. The TRPC2 channel gene, essential for VNO signaling, is also a pseudogene in humans. The consensus is the human VNO is non-functional. |
| 10 | Jun 9 | BSE | `BSE_260609_combinatorial-coding` | **A Code, Not a Label: How Your Brain Encodes 10,000 Smells** | Single odorant activates multiple receptors; single receptor responds to multiple odorants (Malnic, Hirono, Sato, & Buck, 1999, *Cell*). This combinatorial code allows ~400 human receptors to encode a vast number of odor identities. Different concentrations of the same odorant can activate different receptor combinations, changing perceived smell quality. |
| 11 | Jun 16 | FH | `FH_260616_penfield-olfactory-cortex` | **Wilder Penfield and the Cortical Map of Smell** | Neurosurgeon Wilder Penfield, working at the Montreal Neurological Institute in the 1930s–50s, electrically stimulated patients' brains during surgery. Stimulation of the uncus and medial temporal lobe evoked vivid olfactory hallucinations. John Hughlings Jackson had earlier (1880s) described "uncinate fits" — epileptic seizures from temporal lobe lesions — that began with an olfactory aura, often described as a "dreamy state" with a strange smell. |
| 12 | Jun 23 | BSE | `BSE_260623_types-of-smell-loss` | **Anosmia, Parosmia, Phantosmia: A Guide to Smell Disorders** | Anosmia = total loss. Hyposmia = reduced. Parosmia = distorted (e.g., coffee smells like sewage). Phantosmia = smelling something that isn't there. Causes include viral infection (including COVID-19), head trauma, nasal polyps, neurodegenerative disease. Post-viral olfactory loss is the most common cause. Estimated 5% of the general population has anosmia (Bramerson et al., 2004). |

### Phase 2: July–September 2026 (12 articles)

| Week | Date | Cat | Proposed File Name | Title | Key Facts & Sources |
|------|------|-----|--------------------|-------|---------------------|
| 13 | Jul 7 | FH | `FH_260707_olfactory-ensheathing-cells` | **The Healing Cells in Your Nose: Olfactory Ensheathing Cells** | Olfactory ensheathing cells (OECs) were first characterized by Francisco Ramon-Cueto and Manuel Nieto-Sampedro in the early 1990s. These glial cells guide olfactory axons from the periphery into the CNS — unique among adult neurons. OECs have been investigated for spinal cord injury repair, with early clinical trials in the 2000s showing mixed but promising results. |
| 14 | Jul 14 | BSE | `BSE_260714_smell-training` | **Smell Training: Can You Exercise Your Nose Back to Health?** | Proposed by Thomas Hummel (TU Dresden) in a 2009 study (*Laryngoscope*). Patients sniff 4 strong odors (rose, eucalyptus, lemon, cloves) twice daily for 12+ weeks. The mechanism involves stimulating neuroplasticity in the olfactory epithelium and bulb. A 2015 meta-analysis (Pekala et al.) confirmed modest but significant improvement in olfactory function. The protocol remains the standard clinical recommendation for post-viral olfactory loss. |
| 15 | Jul 21 | FH | `FH_260721_ors-outside-nose` | **Smell Receptors Where You Least Expect Them** | In 2003, Hanns Hatt's lab (Ruhr University Bochum) found olfactory receptors in human sperm (OR1D2, responding to bourgeonal — a lily-of-the-valley scent) that guide sperm chemotaxis. Since then, ORs have been found in kidneys, gut, skin (OR2AT4, responding to Sandalore, promotes wound healing — Busse et al., 2014), and even tumors. These are called "ectopic" olfactory receptors. |
| 16 | Jul 28 | BSE | `BSE_260728_piriform-cortex` | **The Piriform Cortex: Where Smell Becomes Meaning** | Three-layered paleocortex — one of the oldest cortical structures. Unlike vision/hearing, olfaction has no thalamic relay before reaching cortex (though the thalamus mediates conscious odor awareness via the mediodorsal nucleus). Piriform cortex performs pattern completion — can recognize an odor even from partial receptor input. Projects to orbitofrontal cortex (conscious identification), amygdala (emotion), hippocampus (memory), and hypothalamus (feeding behavior). |
| 17 | Aug 4 | FH | `FH_260804_perfume-revolution` | **Grasse to the Lab: How Synthetic Chemistry Transformed Perfumery** | Grasse, France, has been the perfume capital since the 16th century (originally for tanning leather gloves). In 1882, Paul Parquet created *Fougère Royale* for Houbigant, using coumarin — one of the first perfumes with a synthetic ingredient. In 1921, Ernest Beaux created Chanel No. 5 using aldehydes. These synthetics democratized perfumery by making scents reproducible and affordable. |
| 18 | Aug 11 | BSE | `BSE_260811_genetic-variation-smell` | **Why You Smell Differently Than Your Neighbor** | OR7D4 gene variants determine whether androstenone (found in pork/sweat) smells offensive, pleasant, or odorless (Keller et al., 2007, *Nature*). ~50% of people cannot smell asparagus metabolites in urine (Pelchat et al., 2011). Humans have ~400 functional OR genes but also ~400 OR pseudogenes. Each person has a unique combination of OR variants, creating individual "odor blindness" patterns (specific anosmias). |
| 19 | Aug 18 | FH | `FH_260818_covid-anosmia` | **COVID-19 and the World's Sudden Interest in Smell Loss** | COVID-related anosmia first widely reported in early 2020 (Giacomelli et al., *Clinical Infectious Diseases*, 2020). Studies showed 40–80% of COVID patients experienced some olfactory dysfunction. Brann et al. (2020, *Science Advances*) showed SARS-CoV-2 infects sustentacular (supporting) cells in the olfactory epithelium, not the neurons themselves, via ACE2 receptors. The pandemic led to unprecedented public awareness and research funding for smell disorders. |
| 20 | Aug 25 | BSE | `BSE_260825_trigeminal-chemesthesis` | **The Other Nose Nerve: Trigeminal Chemesthesis** | The trigeminal nerve (CN V) detects irritants independently of olfaction: menthol → TRPM8 (cooling), capsaicin → TRPV1 (burning), mustard oil → TRPA1. Most "strong" odors (ammonia, vinegar) also stimulate the trigeminal nerve. About 70% of odorants co-activate trigeminal fibers. Anosmics often retain trigeminal sensation, which is why they can still "feel" ammonia. Discovered through early work by W.S. Cain and colleagues in the 1980s. |
| 21 | Sep 1 | FH | `FH_260901_lancet-olfactory-genome` | **Mapping the Olfactory Genome: Doron Lancet's Legacy** | Doron Lancet (Weizmann Institute) was among the first to propose that olfactory receptors belonged to the G-protein coupled receptor (GPCR) superfamily (Lancet, 1986, *Biochemistry*). His lab created the Human Olfactory Receptor Data Explorer (HORDE) database. Humans have ~396 functional OR genes and ~425 pseudogenes (Niimura & Nei, 2003). The OR gene family is the largest gene family in the human genome. |
| 22 | Sep 8 | BSE | `BSE_260908_retronasal-olfaction` | **The Smell You Don't Sniff: How Retronasal Olfaction Creates Flavor** | Retronasal = odor molecules travel from the mouth up through the nasopharynx during chewing/swallowing. The brain processes retronasal signals differently from orthonasal (sniffing) — Small et al. (2005, *Neuron*) showed distinct fMRI activation patterns. Gordon Shepherd coined "neurogastronomy" to describe how the brain constructs flavor. Wine tasters and food experts heavily rely on this pathway. |
| 23 | Sep 15 | FH | `FH_260915_broca-olfactory-brain` | **Paul Broca and the "Smell Brain" Label That Stuck** | Paul Broca (1878) coined the term *le grand lobe limbique* and classified mammals by the relative size of their olfactory structures — "osmatic" (keen smell, like dogs) vs "anosmatic" (weak smell, like humans). This perpetuated the myth that humans have a poor sense of smell. John McGann debunked this in a 2017 *Science* paper ("Poor human olfaction is a 19th-century myth"), showing humans have ~400 functional ORs and discriminate at least a trillion stimuli (Bushdid et al., 2014 — though this estimate is debated). |
| 24 | Sep 22 | BSE | `BSE_260922_adaptation-habituation` | **Why You Stop Smelling Your Own Perfume: Adaptation & Habituation** | Peripheral adaptation: receptor desensitization via Ca²⁺-calmodulin feedback within seconds. Central habituation: decreased piriform cortex response over minutes. These are distinct mechanisms — adaptation is at the receptor/neuron level, habituation is cortical. Cross-adaptation: exposure to one odorant can reduce sensitivity to chemically similar ones. Recovery from peripheral adaptation takes seconds to minutes; central habituation can last much longer. |

### Phase 3: October–December 2026 (12 articles)

| Week | Date | Cat | Proposed File Name | Title | Key Facts & Sources |
|------|------|-----|--------------------|-------|---------------------|
| 25 | Oct 6 | FH | `FH_261006_shepherd-neurogastronomy` | **Gordon Shepherd and the Birth of Neurogastronomy** | Gordon Shepherd (Yale) published *Neurogastronomy* in 2012, arguing that flavor is largely created by the brain, not the tongue. He emphasized retronasal olfaction, cortical processing, and the role of sniffing as an active motor act. His earlier work on the olfactory bulb circuit (1960s–70s with Tom Powell and Charles Greer) was foundational. He showed the bulb processes information like a cortical microcircuit. |
| 26 | Oct 13 | BSE | `BSE_261013_sniffing-as-behavior` | **The Science of the Sniff: Active Sampling in Olfaction** | Sniffing is a motor act synchronized with neural processing. Sniff frequency: ~1.5 Hz during exploration. Noam Sobel's lab (Weizmann Institute) showed humans adjust sniff vigor within 150 ms of detecting an odorant (Johnson et al., 2003). Sniffing alone (without odor) activates the piriform cortex. In rodents, the sniffing rhythm entrains olfactory bulb oscillations — Wachowiak & Bhatt (2004) demonstrated this coupling. |
| 27 | Oct 20 | FH | `FH_261020_monell-center` | **The Monell Chemical Senses Center: A Lab Dedicated Entirely to Smell and Taste** | Founded in 1968 in Philadelphia by Morley Kare, with funding from Ambrose Monell. It is the world's only independent research center focused solely on the chemical senses. Key scientists who worked there include Gary Beauchamp (infant taste preferences), Beverly Cowart (smell disorders), Pamela Dalton (odor perception), and George Preti (body odor chemistry). It has been instrumental in translating basic chemosensory science into clinical and food applications. |
| 28 | Oct 27 | BSE | `BSE_261027_age-related-smell-decline` | **Presbyosmia: Why Smell Fades With Age** | Smell identification begins declining after age 60. Doty et al. (1984, *Science*) tested 1,955 subjects with UPSIT and showed clear age-related decline. By age 80, roughly 75% have major olfactory impairment. Causes: thinning of olfactory epithelium, reduced mucosal blood flow, ossification of cribriform plate openings, reduced bulb volume. Smell loss in aging is also a risk factor for malnutrition and an early biomarker for Alzheimer's and Parkinson's. |
| 29 | Nov 3 | FH | `FH_261103_cloquet-osphresiologie` | **Hippolyte Cloquet's *Osphresiologie* (1821): The First Smell Textbook** | French physician Hippolyte Cloquet published *Osphresiologie, ou Traité des Odeurs, du Sens et des Organes de l'Olfaction* in 1821 — likely the first comprehensive monograph devoted to smell. It covered anatomy, physiology, pathology, and even the cultural significance of odor. The book cataloged smell disorders and proposed classification systems, predating Zwaardemaker by 70 years. |
| 30 | Nov 10 | BSE | `BSE_261110_mucus-obps` | **The Unsung Hero: How Nasal Mucus Makes Smell Possible** | Odorant binding proteins (OBPs) were first identified in cow nasal mucus by Pelosi & Baldaccini (1981). OBPs are small lipocalin proteins that solubilize hydrophobic odorants and transport them through the aqueous mucus to receptors. The mucus also contains enzymes (cytochrome P450s, glutathione transferases) that metabolize odorants — essentially "clearing" the signal to prepare for the next sniff. The mucus layer is continuously renewed. |
| 31 | Nov 17 | FH | `FH_261117_helen-keller-smell` | **Helen Keller and the Power of Compensatory Smell** | In her 1908 essay *"The World I Live In"*, Helen Keller described distinguishing people, places, and seasons by smell alone. She wrote: "Smell is a potent wizard that transports you across thousands of miles and all the years you have lived." Modern research supports compensatory plasticity — studies by Kupers et al. (2011) showed that blind individuals have enhanced olfactory discrimination, with fMRI showing recruitment of visual cortex for smell processing. |
| 32 | Nov 24 | BSE | `BSE_261124_smell-emotion-amygdala` | **Why Bad Smells Make You Gag: The Amygdala-Olfaction Connection** | Olfaction is the only sense with a direct synaptic path to the amygdala (no obligate thalamic relay). Anderson et al. (2003, *Nature Neuroscience*) showed the amygdala encodes odor intensity/valence independent of identity. The orbitofrontal cortex handles odor identity. Disgust responses to odors are faster than for visual stimuli — likely an evolutionary defense against toxins and pathogens. |
| 33 | Dec 1 | FH | `FH_261201_doty-upsit-story` | **Richard Doty and the Test That Changed Clinical Smell Assessment** | Richard Doty (University of Pennsylvania) developed the UPSIT (University of Pennsylvania Smell Identification Test) in 1984 — a scratch-and-sniff test with 40 microencapsulated odorants. It was the first reliable, standardized clinical smell test. His lab then showed that olfactory loss predicts Parkinson's (Doty, 2012) and Alzheimer's pathology. The UPSIT has been used in >100 countries and translated into >30 languages. |
| 34 | Dec 8 | BSE | `BSE_261208_olfactory-epithelium` | **The Olfactory Epithelium: A Self-Renewing Sensory Sheet** | Located on the roof of the nasal cavity, covering ~2–4 cm² in humans (~10 cm² in dogs). Contains ~6 million olfactory sensory neurons (vs ~200 million in dogs). Three cell types: sensory neurons, sustentacular (support) cells, and basal cells (the stem cells). Basal cells (globose and horizontal) replace neurons every 30–60 days — one of the only examples of ongoing neuronal replacement in the adult human body. |
| 35 | Dec 15 | FH | `FH_261215_mombaerts-glomerular-map` | **Peter Mombaerts and the Glomerular Address Code** | Peter Mombaerts (Rockefeller University, later Max Planck) developed gene-targeting techniques in mice to label individual olfactory receptor populations with fluorescent markers. His 1996 *Cell* paper (with Bhatt, Wang, Bhatt et al.) showed that neurons expressing the same receptor project their axons to specific, stereotyped glomeruli in the olfactory bulb — establishing the "one receptor, one glomerulus" rule. This created the first spatial map of olfactory input. |
| 36 | Dec 22 | BSE | `BSE_261222_smell-sleep-circadian` | **Smell While You Sleep? Olfaction and the Circadian Rhythm** | Olfactory sensitivity fluctuates with circadian rhythm — peak sensitivity in the evening, lowest in the morning (Nordin et al., 2003). During sleep, olfactory stimuli generally do NOT cause awakening (unlike auditory stimuli), which is why smoke detectors use sound, not scent. However, Arzi et al. (2012, *Nature Neuroscience*) showed that odor-conditioning during sleep can modify sniffing behavior, demonstrating some olfactory processing persists during sleep. |
